How to use this document This is the authoritative reference for the PWR Simulation educational model. It documents what the model computes, what it simplifies, and what it deliberately does not attempt. Teachers, parents, and administrators can rely on the Limitations, Disclaimers, and AI Disclosure sections (§5, §6, §9) for a full picture of the model's boundaries and its development process.

§1Overview

What this is, what it isn't, and who it's for.

PWR Simulation is a single-file HTML educational simulation of a pressurized-water reactor, designed for Grade 10 classroom use. It teaches reactor dynamics through four control divisions (rods, coolant pump, pressurizer, boron), four primary readouts (hot-leg coolant temperature, system pressure, grid power, keff), and — in Advanced Mode — five nuclear instrumentation readouts (neutron count rate, dose rate, reactor period, reactivity, thermal flux).

The application is delivered as a single self-contained HTML file, hosted on GitHub Pages, licensed under the GNU Affero General Public License v3.0. No build step, no bundler, no server-side component.

1.1 What this model is

  • A teaching tool for reactor dynamics, criticality, and control coordination.
  • An instrument-reading exercise: students learn to interpret cps, dose, period, and reactivity.
  • A safe sandbox for exploring the consequences of control decisions — including failure modes.
  • An honest approximation with disclosed simplifications.

1.2 What this model is not

  • Not a validated plant simulator.
  • Not suitable for real-plant operation, operator training, licensing, or safety analysis.
  • Not a substitute for a licensed training facility.
  • Not intended to reproduce any actual reactor's specific behavior.

1.3 Who it's for

  • Grade 10 students — through worksheet-guided exercises and free exploration.
  • Teachers — running a classroom session on a projector with up to four student phones.
  • Advanced students — exploring the model with the nuclear instrumentation visible and the CSV recorder running.
  • Administrators and reviewers — reading the Limitations and Disclaimers for a full disclosure of the model's boundaries.

§2Quick Start

One sentence per mode.

2.1 Group Mode

Projector host + up to four student phones. The host screen runs the reactor; each phone controls one division (rods, coolant pump, pressurizer, boron). Students join via QR code or 4-digit join code. The reactor must stay within the power band and within thermal limits, with controls unchanged, for the scenario's hold duration. Each scenario ends in a single win or loss — there is no running score. Three difficulty scenarios: Easy, Moderate, Hard.

2.2 Sandbox Mode

Single screen, all four controls enabled, no timer, no win condition. Ideal for demonstrating dynamics to a class without the pressure of a scored scenario. Fails into the same melt / over-pressure / temperature overlays as Group Mode — the physics is real even when there is no score.

2.3 Learning Mode

Clickable reactor schematic. Each component (core, fuel, rods, boron, pump, pressurizer, steam generator, turbine, condenser, hot/cold leg, containment, DNB) opens an explanatory panel. The phase-map chip opens the IAPWS-95 saturation overlay.

2.4 Advanced Mode

Nuclear instrumentation, CSV data recorder, and (planned) guided experiments. Free Exploration shows five additional readouts — neutron count rate, dose rate, reactor period, reactivity, thermal flux — and lets students record all state at 1 Hz for offline analysis. The recorder exports a 14-column CSV that opens in Excel or Google Sheets.

§3Learning Objectives

What students should be able to do after working with the model.

  1. Explain how inserting or withdrawing control rods changes the neutron balance and reactivity.
  2. Read hot-leg temperature, system pressure, and grid power, and identify when each is approaching a limit.
  3. Recognize criticality, subcriticality, and supercriticality via keff and describe what each means for reactor power.
  4. Coordinate across the four divisions to reach and hold a target power level.
  5. Operate within safe limits and recognize failure modes (over-pressure, over-temperature, xenon pit, coolant boiling).
  6. Explain why a pressurized-water reactor's coolant does not boil during normal operation.
  7. (Advanced) Read neutron count rate and estimate reactor power.
  8. (Advanced) Interpret reactor period, reactivity, and dose rate, and explain the difference between prompt and delayed effects.

§4Physics Modeled

Per subsystem: what's in, what's simplified.

4.1 Neutron balance

One-group keff from a normalized channel weight balance: fuel capture, structural capture, rod absorption (S-curve worth), soluble boron, xenon, and leakage. Three feedback channels are modeled, all negative: moderator temperature (about −3.3 pcm/°C, within the real PWR range of 0 to −80 pcm/°C but on the weak end), coolant density / void, and Doppler (proportional to fuel–coolant ΔT). Real PWRs are designed with a negative moderator temperature coefficient for self-stabilization; this model matches that behavior. The channel weights are calibrated for classroom playability, not physical accuracy.

4.2 Point kinetics

Six delayed-neutron groups with realistic β and λ values, integrated by RK4 over 100 substeps per model-second. The prompt neutron generation time Λ is intentionally stretched 1000× (0.10 s vs. real ~1×10⁻⁴ s) so that students can watch the reactor respond on human-observable timescales. For periods longer than ~100 s, the model matches theory. For short periods (<10 s), the response is qualitatively correct but numerically distorted.

4.3 Xenon and iodine

Normalized I-135 / Xe-135 chain. Iodine decay is compressed ~570× and xenon decay ~96× for classroom pacing. Post-SCRAM from full-power equilibrium, the model's xenon inventory peaks about 70 seconds after shutdown — real xenon peaks 9–10 hours after shutdown. The peak overshoot is ~17% above the full-power equilibrium value, so the model's "xenon pit" is a mild excursion rather than a dramatic peak. The pedagogical value — showing that shutdown is not a reset button, that restart may be blocked by xenon — is preserved by the compressed timescales, not by an exaggerated peak. Note that the Hard scenario's starting xenon (1.4) exceeds what the model's own dynamics can produce (~1.03 from full-power equilibrium); this is a scenario setup, not an evolved physical state.

4.4 Thermal-hydraulic

Coolant temperature updated from fission power minus pump-driven heat removal, with a first-order lag. Fuel temperature is computed internally (first-order lag toward Tcoolant + (Nf+20)/3.33) and used as the driver of Doppler feedback, but is not shown as a separate readout — the model does not compute cladding temperature, DNB, or critical heat flux. Pressure updated from a linearized model: heater and spray inputs, plus a temperature term. The pressurizer relief valve is a game parameter (opens at 17.2 MPa, reseats at 16.4 MPa).

4.5 Saturation and phase map

Wagner–Pruss vapor-pressure correlation for water, inverted numerically to compute saturation temperature at a given pressure. The phase-map overlay in the app is accurate. Reference: IAPWS-95 formulation. Above the critical point (373.946 °C, 22.064 MPa) there is no distinct liquid-vapor boundary.

4.6 Grid power

33% thermal-to-electric conversion, minus 5% station service. Fixed; real plants vary. At 1000 MWth thermal, the model delivers ~315 MWe to the grid.

4.7 Nuclear instrumentation (Advanced Mode)

Neutron count rate: 10⁹ cps at full power, proportional to fission rate (single detector across all ranges). Dose rate: four-component sum of prompt γ, N-16 activation (7.1 s half-life), delayed γ from precursors, and fission product γ (single-bucket pool with a 1-hour time constant; compressed from the multi-year decay of a real fission-product inventory, but longer than a single 10-minute scenario, so the pool appears nearly constant during classroom play — correctly conveying that shutdown does not clear residual dose). Reactor period: Nf / (dNf/dt), smoothed over 5 samples, displayed as ±s or +∞ (stable). Reactivity: ρ = (keff − 1)/keff in pcm. Thermal flux: 3×10¹³ n/cm²·s at full power (secondary reading).

§5Limitations

Full disclosure list. Every simplification must be visible to teachers and reviewers.

  1. Prompt neutron generation time stretched 1000× for pacing (Λ = 0.10 s vs. real ~1×10⁻⁴ s). Short-period transients give qualitatively correct shape but wrong numerical reactivity.
  2. Boron worth is realistic. The model gives approximately −10 pcm/ppm over the 0–2000 ppm range, comparable to real PWRs (NRC startup test data: 6.85 pcm/ppm at 2,164 ppm; textbook worked examples: ~−11 pcm/ppm). The boronCapture channel weight (0.47) is one term in the neutron balance, not a pcm/ppm value — do not confuse the two.
  3. Doppler feedback is scenario-tuned. Easy gives ~1850 pcm at full power (above the real ~500–1,500 pcm range, chosen for playability — a strong negative feedback makes the classroom reactor easier to stabilize). Moderate gives ~990 pcm (in real range). Hard gives ~620 pcm (in real range, low end). The ALPHA_DOPPLER constant in the code is a fallback default; each scenario supplies its own alphaDoppler value.
  4. Xenon time constants compressed. Iodine decay ~570×, xenon decay ~96×. Real xenon peaks 9–10 hours after shutdown; the model peaks ~70 seconds after SCRAM, with a ~17% overshoot above equilibrium. The Hard scenario's starting xenon (1.4) exceeds what the model's dynamics can reach (~1.03 max) — this is a scenario setup, not a physical state.
  5. No spatial dimension. Flux, dose, and power are core-averages. Rod tilt, radial peaking, and axial offset are not modeled.
  6. Single count-rate detector across all power ranges. Real reactors use three overlapping instruments (Source Range, Intermediate Range, Power Range).
  7. Dose assumes a fictional shielded position near primary piping. Real detector positions vary by orders of magnitude.
  8. Fission product pool is a single bucket, not an isotope-by-isotope inventory (~300 isotopes in a real plant).
  9. Activation products (Co-60, Fe-59) are not modeled. Real plants retain residual dose for years after shutdown.
  10. Fixed 20 MWth residual heat allowance at zero fission power. Real decay heat falls with time over hours to years.
  11. 33% thermal-to-electric conversion, 5% station service are fixed. Real plants vary with load, coolant temperature, and turbine efficiency.
  12. No DNB, critical heat flux, or DNBR. Coolant failure is a simplified game limit, not a physical boiling crisis calculation.
  13. All warning, hold, and failure limits are game parameters — not real plant settings, not extracted from any regulatory or operational document.
  14. Reactivity uses a one-group keff, not a real eigenvalue. Absolute reactivity numbers are approximations, not measurements.
  15. Period reading is smoothed over 5 samples (~5 s at 1 Hz). Fast transients (period <5 s) are not resolvable.
  16. The simulation is an educational model, not a validated plant simulator.

§6Disclaimers

Legal and safety framing.

This is an educational model, not a validated plant simulator. It is not suitable for real-plant operation, operator training, licensing, or safety analysis. All numbers shown in the application — temperatures, pressures, reactivities, doses, failure thresholds — are illustrative classroom parameters. They do not represent any specific facility, do not follow any regulatory standard, and are not verified against any real reactor's operating envelope.

Dose values assume a fictional detector position near primary piping. They are not representative of any actual installation and must not be interpreted as predictions of dose at any real location.

The application is provided under the GNU Affero General Public License v3.0 with no warranty, express or implied. See the LICENSE file in the repository for the full text.

Not for classroom use as a safety reference Nothing in this simulation should be used to inform decisions about radiation safety, reactor operation, or emergency response. Real reactor safety depends on engineered systems (shutdown rods, emergency core cooling, containment, shielding) and licensed operator training — none of which this model attempts to represent.

§9AI Assistance Disclosure

Honest accounting of how AI was used in this project.

PWR Simulation is conceived and authored by Emmanuel Calderon. AI language models — primarily DeepSeek and Claude — were used as development tools throughout the build. This section describes the division of labor so the project can be evaluated on its own terms.

9.1 The author's role

The author is responsible for every foundational decision in this project:

  • Project conception — the idea of a Grade 10 classroom PWR simulator, the choice of physics depth, the four-division control split, the projector-plus-phones classroom model.
  • Pedagogical architecture — the learning objectives, the three-mode structure (Group / Sandbox / Learning), the scenario narratives (Morning Ramp-Up, The Evening Surge, Restart in the Xenon Pit), and the balance between challenge and accessibility for the target age group.
  • Physics scope definition — deciding what to model (one-group keff, point kinetics, xenon/iodine, thermal-hydraulic), what to simplify, and what to exclude entirely. Every calibration target started as a human judgement about what the classroom needed.
  • Physics validation — checking AI-proposed values against published reference literature (IAPWS-95, Keepin's six-group data, standard one-group diffusion theory). Rejecting or revising any value that failed the physical check.
  • Every ship decision — reviewing each AI-suggested change, and choosing what to adopt, what to defer, what to reject, and what to redesign.
  • User testing — running the app in browsers, on student devices, and in the classroom, and redefining scope based on what actually happened.
  • Editorial voice — including the limitations list, the disclaimers, and this disclosure section.

9.2 How AI contributed

AI was a productive collaborator on the following tasks:

  • Code implementation — drafting physics helpers, the render pipeline, the recorder state machine, and the About screen wiring, from author-authored specifications.
  • Code review and debugging — reading through the codebase for edge cases and mode-transition bugs at the author's request, and diagnosing symptoms reported by the author.
  • Initial parameter proposals — suggesting starting values for the neutron channel weights, N-16 and fission-product pool coefficients, and dose contribution coefficients. Every suggestion was then validated, tested, and either kept, adjusted, or rejected.
  • Documentation drafting — first-pass structure and prose for this document, the proposal, and the inline About screen content, revised by the author.
  • Design proposals — proposing phasing roadmaps, layout alternatives, and CSV column structures; the author chose which to adopt.

9.3 Bottom line

AI-assisted does not mean AI-authored. Every architectural decision, every physics validation, every pedagogical choice, and every ship decision in this project is the author's. AI accelerated implementation and provided a design collaborator's second opinion; it did not decide what to build, why, or for whom.

The right question for evaluating this project is not "was AI used?" but "was the AI's output validated?" The Limitations (§5) and Disclaimers (§6) document the validation boundary of the physics model. This section documents the development process. Both are honest about what lies beyond.
